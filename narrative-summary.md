# The story of *Decoding Fire with Algorithms*

This account comes from the site's own text and from the scene notes in `index.html`, `views/intro.js`, `views/anatomy.js`, `views/logic.js`, `views/problem.js` and `views/transform.js`.

## 1. The central idea

The site opens with its question: **"What can an old kiln teach us about designing new systems?"**

Its argument is that the Hoffmann kiln, read as a computational system, holds ideas worth carrying into other processes. The intro names those ideas as "heat recovery, continuous processes and resource efficiency."

The site tests that argument on a real problem: drying arecanut in the rain. It rebuilds the kiln, one change at a time, into a proposed dryer called **the Drying Cycle**. Then it hands the question back to the visitor.

## 2. How the story unfolds

The story moves through six stages, each building on the last:

1. **The place.** A real tile works, and the kiln inside it.
2. **The machine.** How the kiln works, part by part.
3. **The abstraction.** The kiln's working rules, named as five computing ideas.
4. **The problem.** Arecanut growers whose drying stops when it rains.
5. **The transfer.** The kiln changed into a dryer, one fix at a time, each keeping one of the five ideas.
6. **The honest close.** What the dryer aims to do, what stays the same and what changes, what is still unknown, and a question for the visitor.

The order follows the argument: see the real thing, understand it, abstract it, find a new problem, apply the abstraction, then say how far the claim goes.

## 3. What each section says, and the job it does

### The opening: the place

- **What happens on screen:** A baked model of the Prabhakar Tile Works shows the real place. The question and its subline sit over it. The view then becomes a blueprint: a scan line, a section cut through the roofs, and the kiln drawing itself on the shed floor. The brief appears: the project unpacks the kiln "as a computational system" so its way of "organising heat, time and resources" can inspire contemporary industrial processes. The camera drops until the drawing matches the live kiln.
- **Its job:** It grounds the story in a real place, states the lens (the kiln as a system), and answers "where is this kiln?" before showing how it works.

### The tour: the machine

There are five steps, one idea each. Each idea is one the later sections build on. A part is named only when that idea needs it, and each card ends on the question the next one answers. The kiln starts cold, as an ink drawing. Colour arrives only when the fire is lit, and from then on colour means heat. Long steps speak in beats: the card's words change at set points while the drawing carries on.

- **A loop of chambers:** One tunnel bent into a loop, worked as eight chambers. "No first, no last, so the firing never has to stop."
- **Tiles that stay put:** Loads stay where they are stacked. The card ends: "So what moves?"
- **The fire moves:** First beat: fuel dropped through the roof catches on the hot tiles ahead, so the fire creeps forward, never relit. Only a clock is shown. Second beat, once the fire has moved on: "About every two hours it enters the next chamber. At any moment, each chamber is at a different stage." The table of stages grows open beside it.
- **One way through:** Told here, just before it pays off. The chimney draws the air, but only one doorway and one damper stand open, so the air has a single route. The damper opens and the route draws itself as the heat-carrying arrow.
- **Heat, handed forward:** The climax, in three beats. The hot gas warms the tiles waiting ahead. Then, with the damper opened at the fire instead, that heat goes straight up the chimney. Last, the fuel figures side by side: "Handed forward, the same heat does its work twice."

What the tour leaves out is kept on the evidence sheet: the no-fans draught, bricked doors, chamber temperatures, and the open question about chambers being unloaded hotter than anyone could work in.

**Its job:** It builds an accurate picture of the machine, ending on its central virtue, before any analogy is made.

### The logic in the kiln: the abstraction

- **The framing:** "Nobody at the tile works calls this computing. But a fire that never stops has to be run by rule, and the rules are ones a programmer would recognise." This marks the section as a reading of the kiln, not an observation.
- **The visual link:** The kiln fades away, but its eight chamber numbers stay and straighten into a row. Every card is drawn in that row.
- **The five cards:**

| Card | What it says |
|---|---|
| **Algorithm** | Three steps, in the same order every time: move the fire on, empty a cooled chamber, stack green tiles in an empty one. |
| **Loop** | After the last chamber comes the first, so the procedure never stops. |
| **Pipeline** | Eight loads at different stages at once. "The loads stay put; the stages move round them." |
| **Forwarding** | What one stage throws away is handed straight to the next, which needs it. |
| **Trigger** | "The fire moves on when two hours are up, not when the tiles are ready." |

- **Why Trigger comes last:** The code notes it is the one idea the dryer will change.
- **The turn:** The five terms scatter, a purple ground rises, and the next question appears: **"What else could this system become?"**

**Its job:** It turns the machine into ideas that can be carried elsewhere, and leaves the visitor with a question that needs a new setting.

### The drying problem: the village

Everything here happens in one 3D village under one camera that never cuts. The problem is told as a small mystery: the words hold the kiln back until the obvious fix has failed. Until then it is only seen, on the horizon, still smoking in the rain. Each slip carries one idea, and big lines are kept for the three turns.

- **The answer arrives:** A single ripe arecanut falls onto the question. The camera pulls back: it is one of hundreds on a roof.
- **The roof:** "In the sun, drying takes up to two weeks," with a day counter. Then, at a close-up of one nut opening: "It is ready when it is hard and the husk peels away." This test is planted here; it becomes the dryer's trigger later.
- **The rain:** **"Unless it rains."** A few nuts are labelled "caught by the rain: spoilt". "So the drying moves indoors, and waits," while the day counter runs on to day 45. Then **"The same nuts, two to three times as long,"** with the two bars (roof against indoors) arriving as the summary.
- **The stall:** From above: "Across the village, every batch waits on the rain." Then a quiet stretch with no words: the camera turns to the tile works on the horizon, labelled only "the tile works, still firing."
- **The means:** As the camera comes down to a courtyard: "Growers already have a fuel, though: arecanut husk."
- **The box dryer:** "The obvious fix: one box, one heater." It fails twice. The air leaves while it could still dry more (labelled "still warm, still dry"). And everything stops to empty and refill, then heats up again from cold.
- **The way back to the kiln:** At dusk, the camera turns to the tile works: **"Brick-makers had the same problem."** Then: in 1858 Hoffmann patented a kiln that "never let its fire go out, and never let its heat leave unused." That line answers both of the box dryer's failures. The camera flies to the tile works and cuts the roof the way the opening did. The kiln drawing returns, the fire makes one lap, the chamber numbers come back, and the five ideas settle into a tray. "Let's take a look at the Hoffmann Kiln again."

**Its job:** It gives the abstraction a real need, shows why the obvious answer falls short, and sends the visitor back to the kiln as the answer.

### The transformation: the transfer

Every step follows the same pattern: try the kiln as it is on nuts, show how it fails, make one change, then light one idea in the tray. A short card defines that idea and says what it means here.

- **Load:** If the chambers held arecanut, the fire's heat is too fierce and its smoke must never touch the nuts. So the fire moves outside, to a husk-burning heater that warms clean air. → **Pipeline**: the batches stay in their bays, and warm air moves round them.
- **Shape:** Arecanut takes days to dry, not hours, so four bays are enough. The ring straightens into a row to fit a farm building, and a return duct keeps it a loop. → **Loop**
- **Route:** A fixed heater would favour the bay nearest to it. So dampers move the air's route along instead: "The heater stays put; the path of the air changes." → **Algorithm**: open the next dampers, then close the last.
- **Hand-on:** The driest air meets the nearly dry nuts first, then cools and takes up water on its way to the wetter batches. "One stream of air does the work of several." → **Forwarding**
- **The step:** The dry bay is shut off and the lead moves on. The batch is taken out and refilled by hand.
- **Trigger:** "The condition of the nuts decides when the system moves on." A grower tests the lead batch by hand, as growers already do. → **Trigger**: in the kiln, a clock; in the dryer, the nuts themselves. Straight after, the text admits what isn't known: how often to check, and how well the test works here.
- **Naming:** The system is named *The Drying Cycle*. It steps through all four of its arrangements. The summary line: "The kiln's logic and structure, reworked for a different material, a different process, and a different trigger."

**Its job:** It carries out the transfer the site promised, and shows that each change keeps one of the kiln's ideas.

### The honest close

- **The aim:** "Back to the rain that started this." The two bars return with a third, dashed bar for the dryer: "a claim to test, not a result."
- **Full circle:** The diagram becomes a plan, then a building beside the grower's house in the same rainy village. "Every roof is bare; one batch keeps drying." Steam rises from one chimney at a time, and a door opens for unloading.
- **The comparison:** The kiln and the Drying Cycle side by side. "Four things carried over from the kiln. Two changed: the heat source, and what starts a step."
- **The open questions:** "This is a proposal." Five questions follow:
  - Does it beat indoor drying?
  - How often must the lead batch be checked?
  - Is the air still dry enough by the last batch?
  - How much husk does it burn?
  - Who would run it, and would neighbours share one?
- **The end:** The camera rises over the village as the rain passes, and the site asks: **"Do you have a problem a system like this could help solve?"**

## 4. How the sections connect

Things carried across sections tie the story together:

- **The tile works itself.** The same baked model opens the site, sits on the village horizon "still firing," and is the place the camera flies back to. Both times its roof is cut away the same way to reveal the kiln.
- **The kiln drawing.** The blueprint hands off to the live kiln line for line, and the village scene hands back to that same drawing.
- **The chamber numbers.** They move from the kiln to the row of cards and back onto the kiln, then become the dryer's bays.
- **The five terms.** They are named on the cards, scattered before the question, gathered into a tray when the kiln returns, and lit one at a time as the dryer keeps each idea.
- **Questions as hinges.** The opening question leads into the kiln; "What else could this system become?" leads into the village; "Let's take a look at the Hoffmann Kiln again" leads back. The final question hands the inquiry to the visitor.
- **Problem and fix in pairs.** The box dryer's two failures match Hoffmann's two virtues: the fire never went out, and heat never left unused. Each dryer change is matched to the specific way the kiln fails on nuts.
- **Rain.** It starts the problem, stalls the village, returns over the finished diagram, and still falls on the built dryer in the closing scene.
- **Consistent colour.** Heat colours appear only with fire. Purple marks whatever does the controlling: the logic, the dampers, the trigger.
- **Labelled evidence.** Chips mark what was observed from growers (F), taken from history (L), proposed (A), simulated (S) or not known (U). The proportion of "proposed" and "not known" rises as the story moves from the kiln to the dryer.

## 5. What the visitor takes away

- The kiln's lesson lies in **how it is organised**, not in its fire. It is a never-ending loop, the stages move while the loads stay put, waste heat is handed to the stage that needs it, and it all runs by a simple rule.
- That organisation **can be lifted out and applied elsewhere**. Applied to arecanut drying, it keeps four of those ideas and changes two things: the heat source, and what starts a step (the nuts' readiness instead of a clock).
- The result is **a proposal, not a proven result**. It has a stated aim and a list of open questions.
- The opening question ("what can an old kiln teach us about designing new systems?") is answered by example. The visitor is then invited to apply the same thinking to a problem of their own.

*Note:* The interactive kiln dashboard (play controls, heat recovery switch, the space-and-time chart) is still in the code. The page no longer reveals it: the story ends on the final question over the village.
